STORY CONTENT

STORY 1: THE MOON CAR Apollo 15 Lunar Roving Vehicle | 1971 | The Moon 8 slides

SLIDE 1 — A Car on the Moon?! Imagine going to the Moon.

You step outside your spaceship. You look left. You look right. There are rocks, hills, and endless grey dust.

You want to explore everything!

But there's one little problem.

Your legs can only carry you so far.

What if you had a car?

Well, astronauts once did!

Image: Apollo astronaut standing beside the lunar rover.

SLIDE 2 — Meet the Moon Car Meet the Lunar Roving Vehicle, also called the Moon buggy!

NASA sent it to the Moon with the Apollo 15 astronauts in 1971.

It was a special electric car built to drive over the dusty lunar ground.

And here's the amazing part: it travelled all the way to the Moon folded up!

Image: Full photograph of the rover on the lunar surface.

SLIDE 3 — A Car That Unfolded Like a Toy! Can you imagine opening a folded chair?

The Moon car was a little like that!

It was packed into a small space inside the lunar lander. When the astronauts arrived, they unfolded it and got it ready to drive.

It had four wheels, a seat for two astronauts, and a steering system.

It was ready for its first Moon adventure!

Image: Diagram showing the rover folded and unfolded.

SLIDE 4 — Why Didn't It Need Petrol? Cars on Earth often use petrol. But the Moon car used electricity!

Why?

Because ordinary engines that burn fuel need oxygen from the air, and the Moon has almost no atmosphere.

The rover used batteries instead.

Each wheel even had its own little electric motor!

Four wheels. Four motors. One incredible adventure!

Image: Labelled illustration of the four wheels and electric motors.

SLIDE 5 — Wheels Made for Another World Look closely at the Moon car's wheels.

They weren't made of ordinary rubber!

They were made from special wire mesh.

Why? Because the Moon's ground is dusty, and the rover needed wheels that could move over it.

The Moon also has weaker gravity than Earth. That helped the lightweight wheels carry the astronauts and their equipment.

Image: Close-up of the rover's wire-mesh wheels.

SLIDE 6 — How Did It Find Its Way Home? Here's a mystery.

The Moon has no GPS like the one in your phone.

So how did the astronauts know where they were going?

The rover used special equipment to keep track of its direction and distance.

It counted how far its wheels had turned and used a gyroscope to help work out which way it was facing.

It was a little like counting your steps and remembering every turn!

Image: Diagram showing the rover's route, wheel turns, and gyroscope.

SLIDE 7 — Hello, Earth! Can You Hear Us? The astronauts wanted people on Earth to see their adventures.

So the Moon car carried a television camera and a special antenna.

The camera sent pictures back home.

People on Earth could even help control where the camera pointed!

It was like having a tiny television studio driving across the Moon.

Image: Apollo 15 rover with its television camera and high-gain antenna.

SLIDE 8 — The Car That Never Came Home The Moon car finished its adventure, but it never returned to Earth.

It was left on the Moon when the astronauts went home.

Its batteries cannot be recharged, so it will never drive again.

But it is still there, resting on the lunar surface.

Imagine visiting the Moon one day and seeing a car parked there for more than 50 years!

Closing question: If you could drive the Moon car, where would you go first?

Image: Apollo 15 landing site or rover on the Moon.

STORY 2: THE ROBOT THAT ASKED A BIG QUESTION Viking 1 | 1976 | Mars 8 slides

SLIDE 1 — Is Anyone Home on Mars? Look at Mars.

It is dusty, rocky, and very far from Earth.

But here's a question scientists have wondered about for a long time:

Could anything be alive there?

Maybe tiny living things hide in the soil.

To investigate, NASA sent a special robot to Mars.

Its name was Viking 1!

Image: Mars landscape and Viking 1 lander.

SLIDE 2 — Meet Viking 1 Viking 1 landed on Mars in 1976.

It was a robot scientist!

It had cameras to look around, instruments to study the planet, and a robotic arm to collect soil.

It couldn't walk around like a person.

Instead, it stayed in one place and investigated the world around it.

Image: Full Viking 1 lander photograph with robotic arm visible.

SLIDE 3 — A Tiny Scoop of Martian Soil Imagine trying to find something so tiny that you cannot even see it!

Viking 1 used its robotic arm to scoop up Martian soil.

Then it placed the soil into special containers inside the lander.

Scientists wanted to find out what was hiding in the dirt.

Could there be tiny living things called microbes?

Image: Viking 1 robotic arm and soil-sampling equipment.

SLIDE 4 — Did the Soil Have a Snack? Here's where the experiment gets really interesting!

Viking gave some of the soil a special mixture containing nutrients.

Think of it as offering the soil a tiny snack.

The nutrients had a special scientific label that helped scientists track what happened.

If living microbes ate the food, the experiment might detect gases they released.

Would the soil take a bite?

Image: Illustrated sequence showing soil sample, nutrient mixture, and gas detector.

SLIDE 5 — A Laboratory on Mars Viking had more than one experiment.

Some instruments heated the soil and studied the chemicals that came out.

Scientists were looking for clues about what the soil was made of.

It was like having a little science laboratory sitting on another planet!

Image: Labelled illustration of Viking's soil-analysis instruments.

SLIDE 6 — How Did Viking Stay Awake? Mars can be very cold, and Viking needed electricity to keep working.

But it did not depend on sunlight!

It carried a special power source that used heat from radioactive material to make electricity.

That meant it could keep working through the night, even when the Sun disappeared.

Image: Viking 1 lander with an illustration explaining heat turning into electricity.

SLIDE 7 — Did Viking Find Life? This was the big question.

After studying the soil, Viking's experiments did not give scientists a clear answer that life existed on Mars.

Most scientists interpreted the results as negative or inconclusive.

But one experiment produced results that scientists still discuss.

The mystery of life on Mars was not solved!

Image: Viking's Martian landscape and soil.

SLIDE 8 — A Robot That Left a Mystery Behind Viking 1 worked for years on Mars.

Eventually, a problem stopped it from communicating with Earth.

Its mission ended, but the questions it helped scientists ask are still important today.

And scientists continue searching for clues about whether life ever existed on Mars.

What do you think?

Could tiny living things have existed on the Red Planet?

Closing question: If you could send one experiment to Mars to search for life, what would you send?

Image: Viking 1 landing site or orbital photograph of the lander.

STORY 3: THE MESSAGE IN A BOTTLE THAT'S STILL TALKING Voyager 1 | 1977 | Interstellar Space 9 slides

SLIDE 1 — A Little Traveller With a Big Secret Imagine sending a message inside a bottle.

You throw it into the ocean, hoping someone, somewhere, will find it.

Now imagine sending that bottle into space!

In 1977, NASA launched a spacecraft called Voyager 1.

It carried a special message from Earth and began an extraordinary journey.

Image: Voyager 1 in space, with Earth far behind it.

SLIDE 2 — A Once-in-a-Lifetime Opportunity Something amazing was happening in our Solar System.

Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune were arranged in a way that gave spacecraft a special opportunity to visit several giant planets.

Scientists planned a journey that could take Voyager close to these distant worlds.

It was like getting a chance to visit several amazing places on one long trip!

Image: Solar System diagram showing the giant planets and Voyager's route.

SLIDE 3 — A Planet-Sized Push! How could Voyager travel so far?

It used something called a gravity assist.

Imagine throwing a ball towards a moving bus.

The bus can give the ball an extra push!

Planets can do something similar with spacecraft.

Voyager flew near giant planets and used their gravity to change its speed and direction.

It was like getting a push from a planet!

Image: Diagram of Voyager flying past Jupiter, with arrows showing its changed path.

SLIDE 4 — A Message From Earth Voyager carried something very special: the Golden Record.

It was a message from Earth!

It included sounds, music, greetings in different languages, and pictures showing life on our planet.

Imagine someone far away finding it one day.

They might discover that a little blue planet once sent a hello into space.

Image: Close-up of the Golden Record and its protective cover.

SLIDE 5 — How Does Voyager Get Electricity? Voyager travelled so far from the Sun that sunlight became too weak to power it with ordinary solar panels.

So NASA gave it a different kind of power source.

It uses heat released by radioactive material to generate electricity.

This power source keeps working even in the dark.

But its energy slowly decreases over time.

Image: Voyager's radioisotope power source with a heat-to-electricity illustration.

SLIDE 6 — The Faintest Hello Voyager sends messages to Earth using radio waves.

But there is a problem.

It is incredibly far away!

By the time its signal reaches us, it is extremely weak.

NASA uses enormous radio antennas on Earth to listen for its tiny messages.

Imagine trying to hear someone whisper from the other side of an enormous country!

Image: Voyager sending a signal towards a giant Deep Space Network antenna.

SLIDE 7 — A Robot That Needs to Save Its Energy Voyager cannot plug itself into a wall to recharge.

Its power supply slowly becomes weaker.

So engineers have to make difficult choices.

They switch off some instruments to save energy and keep other important systems working.

It is a little like turning off the lights in empty rooms to save electricity!

Image: Voyager with labelled instruments showing some switched off and some still operating.

SLIDE 8 — Voyager Has Gone Beyond the Sun's Neighbourhood Voyager 1 has travelled farther than any other human-made spacecraft.

It has crossed into interstellar space, the space between the stars.

But it is still sending information back to Earth.

Its journey continues, even though its home is now incredibly far away.

Image: Illustration showing Voyager outside the heliosphere in interstellar space.

SLIDE 9 — The Journey That Will Outlast Us One day, Voyager will become too weak to send messages.

But it will keep travelling through space.

It will carry the Golden Record, a little piece of Earth, along with it.

Even when its voice becomes silent, its journey will continue.

A tiny spacecraft. A huge universe. One message from our home planet.

Closing question: If you could put one message from Earth on a spacecraft, what would you send?

Image: Voyager travelling into a star-filled sky, with Earth appearing as a tiny blue dot.

STORY 4: THE ROBOT THAT RAN OUT OF SUN Opportunity | 2004–2018 | Mars 8 slides

SLIDE 1 — The Little Robot That Wouldn't Give Up Imagine being sent to another planet with a job to do.

You are only supposed to work for 90 days.

But what if you keep going for 15 years?

That is what happened to a little Mars rover called Opportunity!

Its friends at NASA gave it a shorter name: Oppy.

And it had a very big adventure ahead.

Image: Full photograph of Opportunity, with its name displayed like a character introduction.

SLIDE 2 — Meet Opportunity, the Robot Geologist Opportunity was a six-wheeled robot explorer.

Its job was to investigate rocks and soil on Mars.

Why rocks?

Because rocks can tell stories!

They can hold clues about what a planet was like millions or billions of years ago.

Opportunity was looking for evidence that water had once existed on Mars.

Image: Opportunity on the Martian surface and a close-up of a rock it studied.

SLIDE 3 — How Did Oppy Eat? Opportunity needed electricity to move, take pictures, and study rocks.

But it had no wall socket!

Instead, it used solar panels.

Solar panels collect energy from sunlight and turn it into electricity.

Its batteries stored some of that energy so the rover could keep working.

The Sun was Opportunity's power station!

Image: Close-up of Opportunity's solar panels, with sunlight arrows showing how they work.

SLIDE 4 — The Wind That Saved the Day There was one big problem.

Mars is dusty!

Dust could cover Opportunity's solar panels and block the sunlight.

Scientists worried that the rover would run out of power.

But something surprising happened.

The Martian wind sometimes swept the dust away!

It was like someone cleaning the rover's solar panels for free.

Image: Opportunity's solar panels before and after a dust-cleaning event, if suitable images are available.

SLIDE 5 — How Did It Talk to Earth? Opportunity was very far away from its friends on Earth.

So how could it send messages home?

It had a way to send signals directly to Earth.

But it could also send messages to spacecraft orbiting Mars.

Those spacecraft could then pass the messages along to Earth.

Imagine whispering to a friend who tells a much louder friend to deliver your message!

Image: Diagram showing Opportunity → Mars orbiter → Earth.

SLIDE 6 — A Giant Dust Storm! Then came a huge problem.

In 2018, a giant dust storm spread across Mars.

The sky became so dusty that sunlight could barely reach Opportunity.

Its solar panels could not collect enough energy.

Its batteries became almost empty.

And the little rover could no longer send messages as it once did.

Image: NASA images showing the Martian sky becoming dark during the 2018 dust storm.

SLIDE 7 — One Last Hello NASA's engineers did not give up easily.

They sent more than 600 commands, hoping Opportunity would answer.

They waited.

And waited.

But no reply came.

Opportunity's final signal arrived in June 2018.

In February 2019, NASA officially ended the mission.

After nearly 15 years, the little rover's adventure was over.

Image: Opportunity's final resting place or last communication illustration.

SLIDE 8 — A Little Robot With a Giant Legacy Opportunity was supposed to work for 90 days.

It worked for almost 15 years!

It travelled across Mars, studied rocks, took thousands of pictures, and helped scientists learn more about the planet's watery past.

It may be silent now, but the discoveries it made are still helping scientists.

Well done, Oppy!

Closing question: If you were designing a Mars rover, what would you give it to help it survive a giant dust storm?

Image: Opportunity's final panorama or a wide image of its Martian landscape.

STORY 5: THE HELICOPTER THAT SHOULDN'T HAVE WORKED Ingenuity Mars Helicopter | 2021–2024 | Mars 9 slides

SLIDE 1 — Can a Helicopter Fly on Mars? Imagine taking a helicopter to Mars.

You switch it on.

The blades start spinning.

But wait!

Mars has very thin air. There is much less air for the helicopter to push down on.

Could it really fly?

NASA decided to find out.

And so, a little helicopter called Ingenuity was born!

Image: Ingenuity standing on the Martian surface, with Perseverance nearby.

SLIDE 2 — Meet Ingenuity! Ingenuity was a tiny helicopter built for Mars.

It was so small that its main body was about the size of a tissue box!

It had four special blades, a camera, batteries, and a little computer.

It weighed only about four pounds on Earth.

Small enough to surprise you.

Brave enough to try something extraordinary!

Image: Labelled photograph of Ingenuity showing its body, blades, camera, and landing legs.

SLIDE 3 — The Air on Mars Is Very Different On Earth, helicopters fly by pushing air down with their spinning blades.

The air pushes back and helps lift the helicopter up.

But Mars has only about 1% as much atmospheric pressure as Earth at the surface.

That means Ingenuity had much less air to work with.

It needed very fast-spinning blades to help it fly!

Image: Side-by-side illustration comparing Earth's thicker atmosphere with Mars's thin atmosphere.

SLIDE 4 — Two Sets of Spinning Blades Look at Ingenuity's blades!

It had two sets of blades, one above the other.

They spun in opposite directions.

Why?

When one set spins one way, it can make the helicopter's body want to spin the other way.

The second set helps balance that movement.

This clever design helped Ingenuity stay steady in the air.

Image: Close-up of the two rotor sets, with arrows showing opposite directions.

SLIDE 5 — Who Was Flying the Helicopter? Here's another challenge.

Mars is very far from Earth.

A message from Earth takes several minutes to reach a spacecraft on Mars.

That is too long for someone on Earth to steer a helicopter every second!

So Ingenuity had to fly by itself.

Its computer and sensors helped it understand where it was and keep its balance.

It was its own little pilot!

Image: Ingenuity flying above Mars, with an illustration of its onboard computer and sensors.

SLIDE 6 — How Did It Talk to Earth? Ingenuity was too small to send all its messages directly to Earth.

So it had a helper!

It sent information to the Perseverance rover nearby.

Perseverance passed the messages to an orbiter around Mars.

Then the orbiter sent them towards Earth.

It was like passing a message from friend to friend until it reached home!

Image: Communication diagram: Ingenuity → Perseverance → Mars orbiter → Earth.

SLIDE 7 — Five Flights? Try Seventy-Two! NASA originally planned just five test flights.

Five!

But Ingenuity surprised everyone.

It completed 72 flights on Mars.

It showed that a powered aircraft could fly on another planet.

That was a huge achievement for space exploration!

Image: Collage of Ingenuity flying, landing, and resting on the Martian surface. Make the number 72 visually prominent.

SLIDE 8 — The Final Landing In January 2024, Ingenuity went on another flight.

But something went wrong during its landing.

Its rotor blades were damaged, and it could no longer fly.

Its flying days were over.

Even so, it had already done much more than its original mission expected.

Image: NASA photograph of Ingenuity after its final flight, showing the damaged rotor blade.

SLIDE 9 — A Tiny Helicopter, A Giant Achievement Ingenuity may never fly again.

But it changed what scientists believe is possible.

It showed that future missions could use flying robots to explore places that rovers cannot easily reach.

One day, helicopters or other flying machines might explore new worlds.

All because a tiny helicopter dared to try!

Closing question: If NASA gave you a helicopter for another planet, where would you send it first?

Image: Ingenuity on Mars with Perseverance in the distance, or an artistic image of future flying robots exploring Mars.
